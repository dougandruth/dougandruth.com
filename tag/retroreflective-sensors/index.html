Halloween 2025: Jaws

With this being the 50th anniversary of Jaws, it seemed only sensible to bring back the giant fleece shark. With improvements, of course.

We had three goals: make the shark more animated, add a video projection on the door for an additional scare, and add projections on the house for improved atmosphere. Not everything went to plan, but as the song says, two out of three ain’t bad.

In 2010, our Jaws display had a talking Quint. This year we upgraded the talking skeleton with a cap for his head and a flotation barrel for him to lean against while also hiding the speaker system. He also got his own retro-reflective proximity sensor to trigger the spiel and cycle through his recordings and a 12-pack of Narraganset. The beer, as it turns out, was critical in helping people identify the character. More than one parent was heard saying “Look, it’s Quint. He’s got a ‘Ganset!”

This year the house got a video loop taken from the film opening. We were able to rip a long enough shot of the ocean at dusk that could be looped. The buoy gong relentlessly ringing in the distance with the ocean waves gently lapping, provide simultaneous feelings of relaxation and foreboding. It is part of the genius of the movie: You don’t need to see the shark. You feel the shark.

Having Quint rest against the yellow barrel was another reminder of the shark; If you see the barrel, you know the shark is near. We did, however, have a shark. Actually two sharks. 

The first shark was our own Bruce the Shark, this year outfitted with a pneumatic system to articulate the tail in addition to snapping the jaws shut. This unfortunately was our failure for the year. It was grossly under-engineered and was barely able to move under its own weight once assembled.

The second shark was a projection on the door. It’s pretty amazing what you can buy on the Internet these days. Luckily for us, a shark animation video was readily available. All we had to do was cut it to size and load it in the video player.

If anyone was scared, it wasn’t either of the sharks that did the work. The truly terrifying moment came when the blood curdling scream of the girl being eaten at the top of the film was played at a distressingly high volume. Actually, the audio in the film isn’t that scary. There’s a whole lot of “Oh, God. I can’t believe I’m being chewed upon.” That goes on for a bit longer than you might remember, so it was edited down to the most gruesome shrieks. And with a little gurgle at the end for good measure.

For our own personal amusement, we added a second talking skeleton that quoted Brody saying “That’s some bad hat Harry.” Since it has been used by a TV production company (Bad Hat Harry Productions) in a logo at the end of several shows, we made it play when our 220 trick-or-treaters were on their way out. Good on anyone who got that.

Halloween 2020: Le Petit Chien Qui Fait Caca

Writing the title in French is a poor attempt at classing-up this year’s Halloween theme. You will quickly see past that because this year we leaned in hard on childish humor. There’s no covering it up.

2020 being what it is, it seemed problematic to do something scary. Not that a good scare would be inappropriate. It seemed inappropriate to do the expected or the usual. In a year when nothing is usual, it felt like a little levity was needed. To that point, we give you The Little Pooping Dog.

We had already started building our intended theme: A highly interactive multi-media display with several tactile components. Then it sank-in that anything involving touching wasn’t going to fly. Moreover, Halloween itself was in question. Putting more time and money into a project that would encourage people to group together seemed misguided. Then we saw the candy chute.

Some genius came up with the idea of simply sliding the candy down to kids through a pipe. Not only is sliding the candy more civilized than chucking the candy at them or using a slingshot, the pipe itself can be decorated to make the method of transport more festive! But how to decorate?

At some point we were talking about just putting out a talking skeleton from our last use of a talking skeleton and calling it a day. Never mind that it’s a pirate skeleton. We are way past helping people make sense of things. But what if the skeleton pirate had a skeleton dog with him?

Why this made sense at the time no longer matters. The fact that a skeleton dog could easily be acquired and that the dog was able to straddle the pipe such that the dog appeared to be crapping out candy, appeared to us like comedy gold.

By the way, a wiener dog? It just doesn’t get any better and the suggestion for temperament from more conservative advisors was justifiably ignored.

Let’s get down to brass tacks. If the pirate is going to talk, the dog needs to fart, right? The pirate had already been wired-up with an Adafruit Audio FX sound board. Luckily, the sound board could be programmed to take triggers from more than one input, so it could have one sensor for activating the pirate spiel and another sensor for detecting the passing (out) of candy.

The light weight of the candy put into question the use of a physical limit switch. Since we were already using a retroreflective light sensor for the pirate, it seemed reasonable to use a short-range light sensor for the candy.

By now you’re probably already seeing a problem. The sensor has a reset time of <2.5 ms. The Audio FX board requires a 200 ms pulse to activate playback. If the candy is moving at 2 m/s, a typical “fun size” candy bar is going to pass over the detector for only 20 ms. What we need is a pulse stretcher.

Fortunately, we had an old Olimex PIC-IO board laying around that could be programmed to turn 20 ms pulses into 200 ms; thus, ensuring that each candy bar would trigger the audio. Sure, we might have gotten away with using a capacitor and a couple of diodes, but we are software people by trade and using the microcontroller made absolute sure the dog would fart.

Over the course of the night we got 140 kids – about 100 short of a typical night. Still, it was great to see that many people out in the fresh air. The danger is real, but our neighbors were committed to find safe ways to trick-or-treat. For the parents and kids that ventured out, we hope that the laugh was worth as least as much as the candy.

Halloween 2017: Pirates Part Deux

This Halloween was almost in danger of just being yet another talking skeleton. We were short on time, so building-out a new theme didn’t seem achievable. We purchased a couple more skeletons (you can never have enough skeletons) and built a couple new props including a treasure chest. The Jolly Roger and Pirates of the Caribbean samples we had from the last time we did pirates.

It’s difficult to beat a pirate theme. The story elements are universal. If we had put more effort in it we would not have gotten a better response. The goal is to set the mood with props and then hit all the emotional buttons with lighting and music. There’s no big scare which is good and bad. It’s good that people get the theme. It’s bad that there’s no gag to scare them. The gag is a challenge to achieve and not really something we have ever gotten right.

The talking skeleton was run as a stand-alone prop with its own audio and control system based on an Adafruit Audio FX Mini sound board triggered by a retro-reflective light sensor. The rest of the system ran on the VenueMagic show controller triggered by a light beam sensor. As usual, the audio system was pushing enough power to make you think there were real canons being fired.
